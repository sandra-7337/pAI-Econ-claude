# Production Network Formation, Trade, and Welfare

## Model Family Name
Multilocation Trade with Endogenous Buyer-Supplier Matching

## Canonical Economic Question
How does endogenous formation of domestic and international supplier-buyer links alter trade elasticities, propagation, equilibrium efficiency, and the welfare effects of trade-cost shocks?

## Verified References
- Arkolakis, Costas, Federico Huneeus, and Yuhei Miyauchi. 2023. “Production Network Formation, Trade, and Welfare.” NBER Working Paper 30954, issued February 2023, revised January 2026. [NBER version record](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30954). This is a working paper, not a peer-reviewed journal publication.

## When to Use This Model
- Use it when firms actively form both supplier and buyer links across locations.
- Use it when trade shocks induce domestic as well as international network reorganization.
- Use it for quantitative welfare analysis with matched firm-to-firm transactions and geographic matching frictions.

## Typical Primitives
- Locations (i,d,u), intermediate producers, retailers/final producers, and workers.
- Heterogeneous intermediate producers and monopolistic competition.
- Buyer and supplier relationship-formation efforts (e^B_{ud}) and (e^S_{ud}), combined by a bilateral matching technology.
- Iceberg trade costs (\tau_{ud}), markups, substitution elasticity, matching elasticities, and resource costs of forming relationships.
- State in the static model: the equilibrium set and mass of bilateral links. The multisector extension adds an input-output matrix.

## Timing
1. Productivities, locations, and trade costs are given.
2. Firms choose buyer- and supplier-side relationship-formation effort.
3. Bilateral links are generated by the matching technology.
4. Firms price and trade intermediates over realized links.
5. Retailers produce; goods and labor markets clear.

## Information Structure
Firms know aggregate matching technologies, location characteristics, and distributions of potential partners before investing in effort. Match realization is stochastic ex ante; equilibrium distributions and prices are rationally anticipated.

## Agent Heterogeneity
Intermediate firms differ in productivity and location; locations differ in wages, market size, trade costs, and matching conditions. The multisector extension also allows sector-specific technologies and matching parameters.

## Choice Variables
- Firms choose buyer and supplier search/formation effort.
- Producers choose prices, output, and input purchases conditional on links.
- Retailers choose input bundles; workers supply labor.

## Constraints
- Matching feasibility links the mass of relationships to buyer and supplier effort.
- Relationship formation consumes labor and/or retail goods.
- Production, goods-market, labor-market, and location-level expenditure constraints must clear.
- Bilateral link measures, trade flows, wages, and price indices must be mutually consistent.

## Equilibrium Concept or Solution Concept
A static multilocation general equilibrium with monopolistic competition and two-sided matching. Firms optimize formation effort, pricing, and production; retailers optimize input demand; matching and all markets clear. The multisector quantitative version admits exact-hat counterfactuals because its aggregate link and trade flows have gravity structure.

## Key Equations
Let bilateral matches be represented by a constant-elasticity matching function

\[
M_{ud}=\mathcal M_{ud}(e^S_{ud},e^B_{ud}),
\qquad
\frac{\partial\log M_{ud}}{\partial\log e^S_{ud}}=\eta^S,
\quad
\frac{\partial\log M_{ud}}{\partial\log e^B_{ud}}=\eta^B.
\]

Aggregate trade and link flows obey gravity restrictions of the form

\[
X_{ud}=\frac{\mathcal S_u\mathcal D_d\tau_{ud}^{-\varepsilon}}
{\text{general-equilibrium resistance terms}},
\]

where the composite trade elasticity (\varepsilon) includes both intensive substitution and endogenous-link margins.

For a trade-cost shock, the paper’s ex-ante global welfare statistic has the reduced form

\[
d\log \mathcal W=-\kappa_G\sum_{u,d}\frac{X_{ud}}{Y^W}\,d\log\tau_{ud},
\]

where (\kappa_G) is a verified composite of markups, intermediate shares, matching elasticities, and relationship-formation cost elasticities. For location (i), equation (33) of the January 2026 version implies

\[
d\log Q_i^F=-\kappa_i^{net}\,d\log\Lambda_{ii},
\]

with (\Lambda_{ii}) the domestic intermediate expenditure share and (\kappa_i^{net}>0) the network-adjusted inverse trade-elasticity multiplier.

## Main Mechanism
Lower trade costs raise bilateral sales and the private return to relationship effort. More effort creates links, expands available varieties, and lowers downstream costs. Because firms do not generally internalize matching congestion, business stealing, nonappropriability, and double marginalization, equilibrium networks can be inefficiently sparse; their adjustment can amplify welfare responses relative to a fixed-network benchmark.

## Common Propositions
- Equilibrium link and trade flows have gravity form.
- Endogenous networks change both the effective trade elasticity and the multiplier from trade shares to welfare.
- Appropriate sales and link-formation taxes/subsidies decentralize the constrained-efficient allocation.
- A Hosios-like restriction on matching elasticities is required for constrained efficiency in the relevant benchmark.
- Regional welfare changes can be summarized ex post by the change in the domestic intermediate expenditure share and network-adjusted elasticities.

## Comparative Statics Usually Available
- Lower (\tau_{ud}) raises bilateral links and trade, subject to general-equilibrium wage and resistance effects.
- More elastic matching generates stronger link adjustment and a larger effective trade elasticity.
- More input-intensive relationship costs strengthen network amplification.
- Greater congestion or business stealing widens the wedge between private and social link formation.

## Data Requirements
- Matched domestic and international firm-to-firm transaction data with buyer and supplier locations.
- Bilateral trade, tariffs/trade costs, firm sales, link counts, wages, employment, and sectoral input-output tables.
- Variation in tariffs or transport costs that changes relationship formation.

## Estimation or Calibration
Estimate the trade elasticity from bilateral flows and link responses. Estimate buyer- and supplier-side formation elasticities from the relationship between firm sales and link counts. Calibrate input shares, markups, and formation resource shares. The paper uses Chilean firm-to-firm data and OECD inter-country input-output data.

## Counterfactual Analysis
Use exact-hat algebra to change tariffs or iceberg costs while allowing wages, trade, link formation, and input-output demand to adjust. Always compare endogenous-network and fixed-network counterfactuals at a common calibrated trade elasticity when isolating mechanisms.

## Welfare Implications
Endogenous links may magnify gains from lower trade costs when equilibrium networks are inefficiently sparse and link-formation resources generate downstream benefits. The sign and magnitude are not universally larger than in a fixed network because the effective trade elasticity and initial inefficiencies move the sufficient statistic in different directions.

## Welfare Decomposition
Decompose welfare into: direct resource cost of trade barriers; intensive-margin substitution; extensive link/variety adjustment; relationship-formation resource use; markup/double-marginalization wedges; and matching externalities. Regional ex-post welfare uses domestic-share changes, whereas infinitesimal global welfare additionally exposes initial distortions.

## Common Modeling Pitfalls
- Citing the paper as published; the verified January 2026 version remains NBER Working Paper 30954.
- Treating the fixed-network and endogenous-network models as comparable while allowing different trade elasticities.
- Ignoring domestic network rewiring when shutting down international trade.
- Applying the one-sector sufficient statistic unchanged to a multisector economy with misallocation.

## How to Extend the Model
- Dynamic relationship accumulation and switching costs.
- Relationship-specific quality, contract enforcement, or bargaining.
- Inventories, capacity constraints, and disruption risk.
- Firm entry, multiproduct supply, or multinational production.

## Example Research Questions This Model Can Support
- How much do domestic supplier links reorganize after an import tariff reduction?
- Do endogenous production networks amplify or dampen regional gains from a trade agreement?
- What link-formation subsidies correct the matching externality in cross-border sourcing?

## Closely Related Model Families
Bernard-Moxnes-Saito supplier search; Grossman-Helpman outsourcing; ACR welfare sufficient statistics; Caliendo-Parro multisector trade; Baqaee-Farhi network welfare.

## When This Model Is Not Appropriate
Do not use it when partner identities are fixed, when contractual holdup rather than matching is central, or when the adjustment horizon is too short for link formation. It is also excessive for aggregate counterfactuals with no link data and no network mechanism.
