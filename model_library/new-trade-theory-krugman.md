# New Trade Theory — Krugman (1980)

## Model Family Name
Monopolistic Competition, Internal Scale Economies, and Love of Variety

## Canonical Economic Question
Why do similar countries engage in intra-industry trade, and how do increasing returns, differentiated varieties, and market size generate trade and welfare gains without comparative advantage?

## Verified References
- Krugman, Paul R. 1980. “Scale Economies, Product Differentiation, and the Pattern of Trade.” *American Economic Review* 70(5): 950-959. [AEA article PDF](https://www.aeaweb.org/aer/top20/70.5.950-959.pdf).

## When to Use This Model
- Use it for intra-industry trade among similar countries.
- Use it when internal increasing returns and variety are the central welfare mechanisms.
- Use it for home-market effects under trade costs and asymmetric market size.

## Typical Primitives
- CES preferences over differentiated varieties with elasticity (σ>1).
- One homogeneous factor, labor, and symmetric firms.
- Firm cost (w(F+cq)): fixed labor cost (F) and constant marginal labor requirement (c).
- Free entry, monopolistic competition, country labor supplies, and optional iceberg trade costs (τ).

## Timing
Static. Firms enter, set prices taking the price index as given, sell domestically or abroad, and exit until profits are zero; labor and goods markets clear.

## Information Structure
Complete symmetric information. Each firm is negligible relative to the aggregate price index and has no strategic interaction with individual rivals.

## Agent Heterogeneity
Firms are symmetric except for the variety they produce. Consumers are representative. Countries may differ in market size or demand composition.

## Choice Variables
- Firms choose entry and price; quantity follows demand.
- Consumers choose quantities across varieties.
- The mass of varieties, wages, expenditure, and trade are equilibrium outcomes.

## Constraints
- CES demand and constant-markup pricing.
- Zero profit under free entry.
- Labor-market and goods-market clearing.
- Iceberg shipments use resources; tariffs, if added, generate revenue instead.

## Equilibrium Concept or Solution Concept
A Chamberlinian monopolistic-competition equilibrium with symmetric active firms and free entry. Under trade costs and country asymmetry, the location of varieties also satisfies market access and zero profit.

## Key Equations
CES consumption and price index are

\[
C=\left(\int_{\omega\in\Omega}q(\omega)^{(\sigma-1)/\sigma}d\omega\right)^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)},
\qquad
P=\left(\int_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)^{1-\sigma}d\omega\right)^{1/(1-\sigma)}.
\]

Monopoly pricing and zero profit imply

\[
p=\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}wc,
\qquad
(p-wc)q=wF,
\qquad
q=\frac{(\sigma-1)F}{c}.
\]

In the one-sector closed-economy normalization, the number of varieties is (n=L/(\sigma F)).

## Main Mechanism
Fixed cost makes average cost fall with firm scale; CES demand gives each differentiated variety market power; free entry converts operating profits into variety. Trade expands the market and access to foreign varieties, creating gains even with identical countries and technologies.

## Common Propositions
- Identical countries can trade distinct varieties within the same industry.
- Markups are constant under CES and depend only on (σ).
- Free entry pins down firm scale in the symmetric benchmark.
- With trade costs and asymmetric market sizes, the larger market can host a more-than-proportional share of differentiated production.

## Comparative Statics Usually Available
- Lower trade costs expand access to foreign varieties and alter firm location.
- Larger market size supports more varieties and strengthens the home-market effect.
- Higher fixed cost reduces the mass of varieties and raises firm scale.
- Higher (σ) lowers the markup and changes equilibrium scale and variety.

## Data Requirements
- Bilateral industry trade, domestic sales, product counts, prices, and firm counts.
- Market size, wages, trade costs, and expenditure shares.
- Markup or demand-elasticity evidence if constant CES markups are empirically important.

## Estimation or Calibration
Calibrate (σ), fixed and marginal costs, and trade costs from demand, price, firm-scale, and trade-share moments. Intra-industry trade is consistent with the model but does not identify scale economies or monopolistic competition by itself.

## Counterfactual Analysis
Change market size or trade costs; solve prices, variety masses, firm location, trade, and price indices. Separate conditional variety access from entry and relocation responses.

## Welfare Implications
Real income rises through lower CES price indices, greater variety, and scale. With one factor and symmetric consumers, the baseline has no within-country distributional conflict. Constant markups rule out procompetitive markup changes.

## Welfare Decomposition
Decompose welfare into nominal wage/terms-of-trade changes, delivered prices, variety, and iceberg resource losses. Variety accounting must use the CES price index rather than raw product counts.

## Common Modeling Pitfalls
- Confusing internal and external scale economies.
- Using the model to explain exporter productivity premia despite symmetric firms.
- Claiming procompetitive markup effects under constant-elasticity CES demand.
- Treating an intra-industry trade index as unique evidence for this mechanism.

## How to Extend the Model
- Melitz heterogeneous firms.
- Economic geography with labor mobility and agglomeration.
- Variable markups, multiproduct firms, or sectoral comparative advantage.

## Example Research Questions This Model Can Support
- How much of trade between similar countries reflects variety specialization?
- Does market size create a home-market effect in a differentiated industry?
- How do fixed costs affect product variety and gains from integration?

## Closely Related Model Families
Melitz; Armington structural gravity; spatial economic geography; monopolistic competition.

## When This Model Is Not Appropriate
Do not use the baseline when productivity selection, strategic oligopoly, variable markups, endowment-based comparative advantage, or dynamic entry is the main mechanism.
